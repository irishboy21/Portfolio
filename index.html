Measuring Conductivity with Eddy Currents

Senior Design: A Non-Destructive Way to Read a Metal's Conductivity

EE 4910 / 4920 · Project 18 · Advisor: Dr. Jiming Song
Fall 2026 – Spring 2027
Team of Six

Our team is building a system that measures how well a metal conducts electricity without cutting, touching or damaging it. A vector network analyzer (VNA) sends an AC signal into a flat copper coil. The coil's changing magnetic field induces swirling eddy currents in the metal underneath it.

Those eddy currents push back with their own magnetic field, which changes the coil's impedance. The VNA picks up that change in its S11 measurement. A better conductor changes the impedance more, so we can measure reference samples with known conductivity, build a calibration, and use it to estimate the conductivity of an unknown sample.

How It Works
Eddy-Current Measurement
  1. The VNA sends an AC signal into the coil probe.
  2. The coil's changing magnetic field induces eddy currents in the metal.
  3. The eddy currents' field changes the coil's impedance (S11).
  4. Calibration with reference samples turns that change into conductivity σ.

Why frequency matters: eddy currents stay near the surface. The depth they reach, the skin depth δ = 1/√(π f μ σ), gets smaller as frequency or conductivity goes up. Picking the test frequency sets how deep into the sample we're measuring.

A Temperature Sensor on a Chip

A PTAT Temperature Sensor Designed in 45 nm CMOS

EE 330 Integrated Electronics · Final Project
Spring 2026
With Eric Williams

For the EE 330 final project, Eric Williams and I designed a temperature sensor as an integrated circuit. The output had to rise in a straight line with temperature, from 0 V at 0 °C to 1 V at 50 °C. That's 20 mV per degree.

The sensor uses two diodes of different sizes. The difference between their voltages grows evenly with temperature, but by less than 1 mV per degree. So we designed our own two-stage op-amp, added a voltage divider to cancel the offset at 0 °C, and set the gain to bring the slope up to 20 mV/°C. We took the whole thing through schematic, layout and DRC/LVS checks in Cadence.

My Role

Eric and I split the work and helped each other on every part. I started by reading research papers on how diodes change with temperature, so we understood the PTAT idea before we committed to a design. I worked on [the op-amp sizing / the PTAT calculations / the layout: pick what you led], and I spent a lot of time with the TAs fixing DRC errors and comparing the layout against the schematic to track down LVS problems.

Skills & Knowledge Gained

  • The full custom IC flow: schematic, symbol, testbench, layout, DRC and LVS
  • Sizing transistors from process parameters, plus biasing and op-amp stability (phase margin)
  • How two mismatched diodes produce a voltage that tracks temperature
  • Thinking about layout early. Parts that work in a schematic don't always carry over.
  • Asking TAs for help effectively and being honest about what didn't get finished

Resources Used

  • Cadence Virtuoso, ADE Explorer and Pegasus DRC/LVS
  • gpdk045 45 nm design kit, using parameters we extracted in Lab 7
  • Dr. Geiger's op-amp design notes and spreadsheet
  • ScienceDirect papers on diode temperature sensing
  • EE 330 TAs and classmates. ChatGPT, with instructor approval, to explain diode physics.

What Didn't Go Perfectly

The op-amp's gain came out at about 54 dB instead of the 80 dB we designed for, after we resized transistors to pass DRC/LVS. The top-level layout still had five substrate DRC errors and some LVS mismatches that neither we nor the TAs could solve before finals. Next time I'd pick layout-ready parts from the start and run DRC much earlier.

A Temperature Indicator with Memory

Op-Amp Comparators That Switch Differently When Heating and Cooling

EE 230 Electronic Circuits & Systems · Design Project
Spring 2025
With Eric Williams

The goal was to take the signal from an LM35 temperature sensor (10 mV per °C) and show the temperature with three LEDs. Green is on below 40 °C. Orange turns on above 40 °C, and red joins it above 100 °C.

On the way back down, the switching points are different. Red stays on until 80 °C, and orange stays on until 30 °C. This is called hysteresis, and it keeps the LEDs from flickering when the temperature sits right at a limit. We amplified the sensor signal with a non-inverting op-amp, then sent it into three hysteresis comparators built from LMC660 op-amps. We simulated the circuit in LTspice, then built and tested it on a breadboard.

Design & Build
LTspice / Breadboard

My Role

Eric and I did this project together. We worked out the threshold and resistor math by hand, built the LTspice simulation, built (and rebuilt) the breadboard and measured where each LED switched. [Add the parts you led yourself.]

Skills & Knowledge Gained

  • How comparators with hysteresis (Schmitt triggers) work and how to set their trip points
  • Using an op-amp gain stage to boost a small sensor signal
  • Going from an LTspice simulation to real hardware and comparing the two
  • Debugging. Our first version used transistors as switches and didn't work. With a TA's help, we switched to comparators.

Resources Used

  • LTspice for schematic and simulation
  • EE 230 lab kit, LMC660 op-amps, LEDs and an LM35 sensor (a DC supply stood in for testing)
  • Bench power supplies and a multimeter
  • Gary Tuttle's Comparators notes (Iowa State) and TI's Comparator with Hysteresis Reference Design
  • EE 230 TAs

Result & Lesson

The circuit worked and the LEDs changed in the right order, but the switching points were a few degrees off. We set the comparator reference voltages directly instead of designing proper voltage dividers, and a few resistor values were off. The big lesson was to treat reference voltages as part of the design. I used that later in EE 330.
